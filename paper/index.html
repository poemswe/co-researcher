When AI cites a real paper, does it say what the paper says?

Building a checker to find out.

Why I built this

I build Co-Researcher, research tools that run inside AI assistants such as Claude Code and Codex. One tool writes literature reviews, which summarize what published studies found. AI writers sometimes cite papers that don't exist, and my name goes on what this tool produces, so I wanted every reference checked.

Checking that a paper exists takes one database lookup. Checking what the review says about it takes more. Take the sentence “The trial found no change in recall (Lumen, 2024).” Lumen 2024 can be a real paper and the sentence can still be wrong, because the study found a change, or measured something other than recall, or was written by someone else. A reference check passes all three.

How the checker works

For every study it uses, the AI records what it says the study found, the exact words from the study that support it, and which study it is. Before the review goes out, the checker tests each entry against four rules.

  • The quoted words appear in the study.
  • The citation points to that study.
  • Every number in the claim appears in the quoted words.
  • Every cited sentence in the review matches a recorded claim, with the same numbers and the same “no” or “not”.

When a rule fails, the AI gets the list of failures and another try, for up to three rounds. A review that still fails goes out marked INVALID EVIDENCE. The rules run as ordinary code that compares text, so the same review always gets the same verdict and no second AI is asked for an opinion.

How I tested it

I wrote short, made-up studies so no AI could recognize them from training, and planted one mistake in each test. Claude, from Anthropic, and Codex, from OpenAI, then wrote reviews from those studies. For each test I recorded whether the checker flagged the planted mistake before any fixing.

What still gets through

When Codex reviewed a draft of the technical write-up, it found four kinds of mistake that get a perfect score. I reproduced all four.

  • A claim can say a study “found a change” while its quote says “found no change”, because the checker compares a claim with its quote on numbers only. I'm fixing this one first.
  • An uncited sentence with no number goes unchecked, so “This treatment conclusively cures dementia.” passes.
  • A finding can come from a paper marked as background reading, as long as the sentence states no number.
  • A review can write “60 adults and 40 children” for a study of 40 adults and 60 children, because the checker matches numbers without tracking what each one counts.

The checker catches the mistakes I built it to catch, with real AI writers in the loop. I haven't yet tested it on reviews of real papers. Because I wrote the mistakes and the checker myself, this test is easier than real use would be.
